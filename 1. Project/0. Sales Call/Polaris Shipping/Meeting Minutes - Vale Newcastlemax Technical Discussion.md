## 4. Structural Safety — Damage Stability & Hull Strength (Key Discussion)

> The most substantive technical exchange of the meeting. EVP Jung raised deep, long-standing concerns about the structural adequacy of current classification rules for large bulk carriers, including Newcastlemaxes. BV technical specialists acknowledged the validity of his analysis.

### 4.1 Background — Polaris' Direct Experience with Structural Casualties

Major incidents EVP Jung referenced from direct professional experience:

| Incident | Vessel | Details |
|----------|--------|---------|
| Structural failure / buckling | **Vale Beijing** | Structural buckling; Polaris direct involvement |
| Grounding | **Vale Indonesia** | Large Newcastlemax grounding |
| Sinking | **Stellar Daisy** | Loss in South Atlantic (2017); operated by Polaris |
| Total loss | **Stellar Banner** | Grounding / CTL (VOC class) |

> EVP Jung: *"These are the biggest accidents of my career. Every single one is connected to structural vulnerability."*

- Polaris applies the **strictest condition monitoring standards in the industry** — any hair crack on any vessel triggers immediate repair
  - *"No company checks vessel condition as rigorously as we do. Even a hair crack means repair, immediately."*

### 4.2 The Limits of Mandatory Rules — Core Complaint

EVP Jung's central argument:

> *"If you apply only the classification society's mandatory rules, you will get cracks everywhere — 100%. The standards need to be raised across the board, but every time I raise this with class societies, nothing happens."*

- Mandatory rule compliance alone is insufficient to ensure real structural safety
- Owners such as Vale frequently impose specifications beyond class rules in their own requirements — but these never become mandatory
- **Vicious cycle**: Shipyard competition on price → structural optimization (weight reduction) → cracking and structural damage → risk of major casualties

### 4.3 Fundamental Flaws in Damage Stability Rules

EVP Jung's core argument:

> *"The current Damage Stability rules only assess whether a flooded hold is 'safe or unsafe.' They do not evaluate the overall survivability of the vessel. They look at cargo — not people."*

Specific concerns raised:

- **Span issue**: Excessive distance between the forepeak and cargo holds creates inherent vulnerability — insufficient residual stability after flooding
  - *"The fundamental problem is that the span is too long. That is why Damage Stability is so weak on these ships."*
- **Exemption abuse**: When forepeak-to-hold distance exceeds a threshold, double bottom and longitudinal subdivision requirements are **waived** — removing key safety features
- **Oversized holds**: Combining multiple holds into one oversized compartment makes vessel recovery impossible once flooding begins
- **Cargo vs. human life perspective**: Current rules are calibrated to protect cargo — not to protect lives

### 4.4 Hatch Coaming Wear-Down Allowance — Irrational Standard

- **Current rule**: Hatch coaming wear-down of **1mm** triggers mandatory renewal
- **Comparison**: Single-skin bulk carriers — wear-down allowance of **20%**; tankers — similarly much higher margins
- **Practical implication**: Hatch coaming will require renewal **at least once or twice** in the vessel's lifetime as a matter of course
  - EVP Jung: *"A 1mm margin means you are essentially guaranteed to replace the hatch coaming at least once or twice over the ship's life. That is what it amounts to."*
- Hatch coamings are subject to frequent mechanical damage — the negligible wear-down margin creates a structural contradiction

### 4.5 Korean-Built Vessels — Structural Optimization Risk

- Korean shipyards apply **extensive structural optimization (weight reduction)** at the design stage → reduced scantlings
- Comparative case — H-Line bulk carriers (KR class):
  - KR granted SOLAS equivalency exemptions for **No. 4 and No. 6 ring tanks** — lighter but more prone to long-term deformation
  - Significantly higher crack incidence compared to Polaris' vessels of equivalent type
  - Long-term risk: progressive deformation and cracking → potential major structural failure after 20 years
- Chinese-built vessels: heavier scantlings → relatively fewer cracks
  - *"Chinese-built ships have fewer cracks now. They're heavier and the semi-flow is better."*

### 4.6 PSPC & Actual Wear-Down Margins

- PSPC (Performance Standard for Protective Coatings) has improved coating quality → better wear-down condition monitoring
- However, EVP Jung stated: *"The nominal 20% wear-down margin was never realistic for many of our vessels from the start — the margin was already insufficient at delivery."*
- **Risk scenario**: Progressive structural weakening → sudden buckling (same failure mechanism as Vale Beijing)
  - *"One day it just buckles like Vale Beijing — the frame buckles and collapses. That's what happened."*

## 5. Classification Rule Adequacy & IACS Reform

### 5.1 Prior Submissions & Industry Response

- EVP Jung has formally raised these issues with **KR, Lloyd's Register, DNV, and BV** on multiple occasions
- **IACS-level response**: Passive — BV cited insufficient fleet size; other societies similarly non-committal
- EVP Jung: *"I've raised this with IACS committees. Nobody wants to change the rules. I genuinely cannot understand why."*

### 5.2 Structural Reasons for Inaction

- **Inter-class competition**: If any one society unilaterally raises structural requirements, owners will migrate to less demanding societies → commercial disadvantage
- **IACS consensus mechanism**: Rule changes require unanimous agreement → reform is practically impossible under current structure
  - EVP Jung: *"If one class society raises the bar, owners say it's too expensive and go elsewhere. That is the core problem."*
- BV technical specialist: **fully agreed** with EVP Jung's structural analysis

### 5.3 Polaris' Formal Request for IACS Rule Reform

> EVP Jung's explicit request: *"I want IACS to raise this issue quickly and at minimum revise the rules on Damage Stability and hull strength. Then ships built from that point on will be ones that owners and crew can trust."*

- This goes beyond Polaris' self-interest — framed as a **public safety issue for the entire industry**
- Recommended escalation path within BV: **Olivier Carti (BV HQ, Head of Technical)**
- Target regulations: Damage Stability standards and Hull Strength (scantling) requirements

## 6. Korean Regulatory Context — Stellar Daisy & Legal Implications

### 6.1 Ongoing Korean Government Investigation & Proceedings

- Criminal proceedings ongoing → civil litigation to follow
- Concern: Under intense political pressure, MOF (해수부) and KR are unable to publicly acknowledge structural/design root causes — **crew and operator fault narrative dominates**
- EVP Jung: *"It's a witch-hunt. They focus on punishment and bury the real issue. The structural vulnerability of the vessel type is the primary cause — not the operator."*

### 6.2 BV's Differentiated Position — Positive Perception from Polaris

- Polaris views BV as **free from Korean domestic political pressure**, unlike MOF and KR
  - *"BV can speak freely on this. They're not constrained."*
- KR and MOF are effectively unable to advocate for structural rule reform following the Stellar Daisy incident
- **BV's independent stance** is seen by Polaris as a genuine trust differentiator in class selection

### 6.3 Legal Risk — Insurer Subrogation Exposure

- EVP Jung: *"If the Korean government concludes that it was 100% the owner's fault, the insurer could file a subrogation claim against us."*
- The vessel maintained full class status at the time of loss — forming the primary legal defense; however, insurer aggression cannot be ruled out
- Brazilian port authority, class surveyors, and inspectors all cleared the vessel at time of loading with no objections
- **If the Korean Marine Safety Tribunal verdict attributes fault entirely to the owner**, that finding could be cited verbatim in civil proceedings — highly unfavorable for Polaris

> **EVP Jung's implicit request**: If BV formally raises rule inadequacy at IACS, this would not only advance industry safety but could directly **support Polaris' legal defense position**

## 7. Sales Implications — Class Selection Signals (BV Korea Internal)

> This section summarizes key signals from Polaris Shipping's statements regarding class selection. For BV Korea internal reference only.

### 7.1 Demand for Structural Philosophy Beyond Mandatory Minimums

- Polaris wants a classification society that **proactively safeguards real structural strength and seaworthiness**, not one that merely fulfills IACS minimum requirements
- In BV proposals, leading with **crack prevention track record and Damage Stability enhancement capabilities** will be more persuasive than generic rule compliance claims
- BV's Additional Notations and Enhanced Survey program options could be highly effective talking points

### 7.2 Technology Risk Hedge Partnership

- Polaris is experiencing information asymmetry and anxiety around new technology adoption (RotorSail, WAPS, etc.)
- They want a class society that can **filter credible makers and provide technical direction based on global data**
- Christophe Vaes' Cross-Check proposal already generated positive engagement → timely follow-up with the filtered maker list will be directly linked to winning the contract

### 7.3 Psychological Safety & Legal Protection — Most Decisive Factor

- EVP Jung: *"Even for our newbuilds, we are all on edge."* — Post-Stellar Daisy anxiety is deeply embedded at the executive level
- The classification society that can create an environment where **owners feel legally and structurally protected** will have the decisive advantage
- BV's message that it will lead IACS rule reform goes beyond technical cooperation — it positions BV as a direct contributor to **Polaris' corporate risk management**

### 7.4 BV's Independent Position — Key Differentiator

- Polaris explicitly and positively recognizes that BV is able to make **independent, politically neutral technical judgments** — unlike KR or domestic regulators
- This positioning should be made explicit in ongoing engagement with Polaris
