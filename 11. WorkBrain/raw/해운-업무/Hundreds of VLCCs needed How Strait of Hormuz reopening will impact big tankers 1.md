Analysts are predicting a cargo rush to boost VLCC use as the Strait of Hormuz reopens.

All eyes will be on how quickly missing oil barrels return in the Middle East Gulf, Clarksons Securities believes.

The investment bank sees three phases for tankers when the US-Iran peace deal is implemented: an initial cargo scramble, a short normalisation dip as trapped ships return and a longer inventory rebuild.

Global seaborne crude trade is down around 7m barrels per day since the conflict began, Clarksons estimated.

A return of this volume would require 140 to 150 VLCCs, analyst Frode Morkedal calculated.

And the Energy Information Administration expects oil demand growth of 2.5m bpd in 2027.

Adding a 1m to 1.5m bpd build of oil stocks to this would mean required global oil production growth near 4m bpd, implying at least 100 VLCCs of incremental demand on a Gulf to Asia-equivalent basis, the analyst added.

Fearnley Securities said the timeline of the peace talks points to a signing on Friday in Switzerland.

The US naval blockade will be lifted, and Iran could get sanctions relief and release of frozen funds, based on compliance.

“The deal is yet to be signed, and the situation remains fragile. However, we are moving closer to a resolution of the conflict than we have ever been, which in our view generally should be positive for shipping,” the Norwegian investment bank added.

Clarksons believes the orderbook is the main pushback for VLCCs, with roughly 270 vessels on order, or around 30% of the fleet.

But Morkedal views the replacement requirement as “substantial”.

He noted that around 215 VLCCs, or 23% of the current fleet, are shadow or sanctioned vessels, many with limited relevance in normalised trades.

And the number of VLCCs older than 20 years will reach 265 by 2028, when many newbuildings are due.

Container ships and car carriers are more exposed on the downside if de-escalation reduces the Houthi threat and brings the Red Sea and Suez Canal back into play, the analyst said.

“Operators would likely return gradually and only once safety and insurance risks are acceptable. Even so, with Africa diversions absorbing roughly 10% of the container ship fleet and around 6% of the car carrier fleet, a credible return to Suez routing would act like a supply injection, without any new ships entering the fleet,” he added.

The impact on VLGCs is more nuanced, however.

Recent rate strength has largely been driven by more US Gulf to Asia cargoes routing around Africa rather than through the Panama Canal, where high slot costs and congestion have made the Cape route more attractive, Morkedal explained.

“VLGCs therefore only become clearly negative if Panama Canal usage changes,” he said.

In April and May, only 52% of both laden and ballast legs used the canal, versus a 68% share for laden voyages in 2025.

## Sanctions question

Sanctions easing could also be positive, Clarksons argued.

Unlike crude, Iranian LPG exports rely entirely on third-party vessels as Iran does not own any gas carriers.

According to lobby group United Against Nuclear Iran (UANI), 44 VLGCs are US-sanctioned, while at least 56 have reportedly been involved in the trade.

Before the recent escalation, Iran exported around 11 mtpa to 12 mtpa of LPG.

Assuming a 50/50 discharge split between India and China, this would require 23 to 25 VLGCs against a mainstream fleet of around 374, Morkedal said.

“In sum, Iranian LPG would likely be a demand positive for mainstream VLGCs,” he added.

Fearnley Securities analyst Fredrik Dybwad said: “We interpret [a reopening of Hormuz](https://www.tradewindsnews.com/tankers/-ran-declares-strait-of-hormuz-fully-shut-again-after-new-wave-of-us-airstrikes/2-1-2002433) to provide a boost in earnings for most segments, fuelled by inefficiencies and pent-up demand that may take up to 12 months, or even longer, to work through.”

The investment bank expects tanker and LPG shares to be among the biggest beneficiaries of any breakthrough.

Dybwad argued that weeks of disruption have created substantial pent-up demand while forcing shipowners and charterers to reposition vessels away from traditional trading patterns.

That could leave tonnage availability tight even after the waterway reopens.

“There is a lot of potent pent-up demand, especially within tankers and LPG, while there has been a large repositioning of vessels following the closure of the strait that will make vessel availability scarce,” he said.

He suggested consensus earnings forecasts might still underestimate the upside.
