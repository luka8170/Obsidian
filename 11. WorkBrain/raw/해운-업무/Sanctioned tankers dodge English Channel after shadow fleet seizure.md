The English Channel is free of sanctioned tankers caused by a mass rerouting around the coast of Britain after the first UK seizure of a shadow fleet vessel.

Tankers hauling Russian crude turned around as they approached the Channel in the immediate aftermath of Royal Marines boarding the 106,800-dwt Smyrtos (built 2009) on Sunday and redirecting it to the English coast.

Other tankers have taken the longer route around Scotland and the west coast of Ireland after the UK took its inaugural action on its threat to join European allies in halting ships linked to the Russian oil trade.

Fleeing vessels included the Chinese-owned and operated 50,000-dwt Sona (built 2009). The Sierra Leone-flagged tanker was sanctioned by the European Union and UK last year.

The Cameroon-flagged 116,100-dwt Maini (built 2007) also reversed course before entering the Channel and diverted around the UK coast, according to Kpler ship and cargo-tracking data.

The Indian master of Smyrtos, Ajay Pant, was remanded in custody on Tuesday after appearing in court in Southampton, accused of breaching sanctions. If found guilty, he could face a 10-year jail term.

Prosecutors said his ship entered territorial waters without a legitimate flag. Shipping databases listed the flag as unknown.

European countries have stepped up their seizures of false-flagged vessels in 2026 as part of increasing pressure on the shadow fleet.

At least five ships have been held this year following action by France, Belgium and now the UK.

The ships that have been targeted have been flying false or unknown flags, with some registries claiming to have been targeted by fraudsters on behalf of shadow fleet operators.

European countries have insisted they have the right to board stateless ships under Article 110 of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Seas (Unclos).

It allows for boarding by a warship where there are “reasonable grounds” for suspecting that the targeted ship “is without nationality”.

On Wednesday, only one sanctioned ship was heading towards the English Channel, loaded with more than 700,000 barrels of crude.

The Russian-flagged 115,655-dwt Forwarder (built 2009) was sanctioned by the EU, US and UK in 2025.

It would not be eligible for seizure under Article 110 of Unclos, owing to the Russian flag.

Reports have suggested that the UK believes it can seize ships under domestic sanctions and money laundering legislation, but it is not clear if a vessel also has to be false-flagged or stateless. The UK government has been contacted for comment.

UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer announced in March that the UK was ready to seize ships.

Hundreds of sanctioned vessels have passed through the Channel hauling Russian oil to Asian markets since the beginning of the Middle East war.

Each targeted ship would be “individually considered by law enforcement, military and energy market specialists before a recommendation is made to ministers and an operation is executed,” the government said.

Malin Hogberg, of Swedish law firm WERKS and a former head of the International Group of P&I Clubs sanctions committee, said detaining a master would have limited effect on halting the shadow fleet.

“There must a monetary and sizeable cost to the owners and operators of the shadow fleet ships and not simply criminalization of seafarers,” she said.

“To charge the Masters of these ships is thus a red herring distracting from finding effective measures to go after the owners and operators of these ships.”

The UK government announcement in March led to a number of Russian-linked ships rerouting around the UK. Russia described the threat as a “deeply hostile step”.

On Tuesday, a Russian frigate fired warning shots to divert a UK-flagged yacht in the Channel to prevent a collision, according to Russia’s defence ministry.

An industry source told TradeWinds that the events were likely linked. “This is the Russian navy saying: ‘We’re here off the UK’,” the source said.

A number of tankers switched to the Russian flag after the US boarded and seized stateless ships as part of its blockade of Venezuela.

Russia protested after one sanctioned tanker, the 318,500-dwt Marinera (ex-Bella 1, built 2002) claimed to be flying its flag when it was boarded by US forces in the North Atlantic in January.

But the pursuit started when the vessel was flying the false flag of Guyana and changed allegiance during the pursuit.

One crew member, fitter Maxim Karpenko, later told Russia’s PortNews that he got out his sewing kit to repurpose a Dutch flag on board into a Russian one to fly on the ship.
