Meeting Minutes — Gerrit Bund (BV) x SK Shipping — PTO Optimization & LNG Carrier Tank Design



Topic 1: PTO (Shaft Generator) Optimization for LNG Carrier Engines

Why this topic came up
SK Shipping asked for this explanation because they have already invested in a dual-fuel (X-DF) engine LNG carrier with a PTO (shaft generator) — the first time their company has done this for an LNGC — and they are not confident that the PTO can always be operated efficiently in practice.

Gerrit Bund's presentation

* New LNG carrier designs often use two engines and two propellers (twin-skeg configuration). On each shaft line, owners like to add a PTO (shaft generator) so they can generate electricity directly from the main engine and stop running a diesel generator during normal sea passage.
* Engine choice: MEGI (high-pressure, fuel injected directly into the combustion chamber) vs. X-DF (low-pressure, uses scavenging air to bring gas in). Power output is similar for both (about 29 MW for the 5G70 / X72 engines). The main difference is in emissions control (EGR, variable combustion control) rather than raw power.
* Typical shaft generator (PTO) sizing: about 3,500–3,800 kW total, split into two units of about 1,750 kW each. Compared to the engine's available power (about 29 MW), each PTO only absorbs around 5–6% of the engine's total design power.
* Engine performance is described using a power (kW) vs. RPM diagram. The engine has a maximum continuous rating (SMCR) and a normal continuous rating (NCR) used in daily operation.
* When a shaft generator is added to the line, it adds extra load. Because the engine's SMCR is fixed (set by fuel injection timing and software), the engine can no longer use its full RPM range — it is effectively limited to about 90% of RPM, losing about 10% of available power for the shaft generator's benefit.
* Two ways to fix this loss of engine capacity:
  1. Controllable Pitch Propeller (CPP) — an older, hydraulic solution that is mechanically complex.
  2. Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) — the modern solution. A battery of about 150 kW can restore the same benefit as a CPP by supplying extra power to the shaft generator load, keeping SMCR and NCR at the same point. Scaling up to about 450 kW (roughly USD 145,000 investment) also gives peak-shaving benefits for the diesel generators, not just the main engine. This is described as a good investment with three combined benefits: fuel efficiency, peak power support for the main engine, and peak power support for the diesel generators.
* Key insight on efficiency: the shaft generator only makes sense at certain operating speeds. At high speed (about 19 knots) there is no efficiency gain from the PTO — you put in extra fuel but get nothing back. At reduced/economical speed (about 12–13 knots, roughly 60–76% engine load), the PTO/shaft generator is in its ideal operating range and makes real sense.
* Because of this mismatch, many owners complain that they paid a lot of money for a large shaft generator but cannot actually use it across their real operating speed range — their expected return on investment (originally calculated at 3–4 years) can stretch out to 6–8 years if the generator is oversized relative to how the engine is actually run.
* Recommendations from Gerrit Bund:
  1. If you don't want to invest in a CPP, consider using two differently-sized shaft generators (one small, one big) instead of two identical units — this lets the smaller unit run efficiently across a wider range of conditions, avoiding the "gap" where the generator can't be used at all.
  2. Alternatively, invest in a CPP.
  3. The right combination depends on the vessel's real operating profile — how much time is spent at full speed vs. reduced/economical speed.

Topic 2: 3-Tank vs. 4-Tank LNG Carrier Design

SK Shipping asked Gerrit Bund's professional opinion on 3-tank vs. 4-tank cargo containment design (they had already run their own simulations but wanted his engineering intuition), including whether extra structural (sloshing) analysis is needed for either design, since they were concerned the 3-tank design has less track record and may be less reliable.

Gerrit Bund's response

* Structural strength: Both designs have GTT approval, meaning both are calculated as strong enough for sloshing loads. The 3-tank design has about 28% reserve strength above the minimum required standard. The 4-tank design is even stronger, at about 36% reserve strength (it is heavier because of this added structural reinforcement in the cofferdams). In practice, this extra strength means a 4-tank ship can carry deck cargo (e.g., carbon capture equipment) and operate in a wide range of speeds and conditions without worrying about sloshing.
* His real concern is not structural safety but operational flexibility, driven by the charter/cargo split:
  - With a 3-tank design, loading is less flexible. If a charterer requires, for example, 100,000 cubic meters to Port A and 50,000 cubic meters to Port B, one tank may end up empty while others are full, which can create insufficient structural margin in the cofferdams. This forces the crew to keep cargo below about 30% loading level in one tank, and the vessel may need to purge, warm up, and cool down as many as three times for a single voyage — which costs a lot of operational time.
  - A 4-tank design allows more even, flexible cargo splits, and can discharge roughly twice as fast in these split-delivery scenarios.
  - "Time is money" — the operational time lost to purging/warming/cooling cycles is the main cost of the 3-tank design's inflexibility, not structural risk.
* Cargo pump reliability (a practical lesson he shared): running two cryogenic pumps simultaneously at reduced flow (to split a required total flow) causes pump vibration and can damage the bearings within about 150 running hours, because the pump curve behaves poorly away from its 100% design point. His strong recommendation: always run pumps at their 100% design flow (about 2,600 cubic meters/hour per pump) rather than running two pumps at partial output — this is both more energy efficient (full engine/shaft generator efficiency) and better for pump/bearing life. If more combined flow is truly needed, connect all pump lines to a single manifold header rather than running each pump at a reduced rate.
* Long-term market context: LNG carriers are typically contracted for about 20 years (a ship worth around USD 280 million), but the market and supply routes can change quickly — he cited how the closure of the Strait of Hormuz during the Iran conflict forced buyers to source gas from Qatar to instead come from Australia, Indonesia, and Africa. New competitors to LNG (ammonia, biomethane blending) are also emerging. This means route and cargo flexibility can matter more over a ship's life than originally planned.
* Bottom line / recommendation: the right choice depends on the charter type.
  - A fixed, long-term contract on a single route (e.g., a 20-year Qatar-to-China contract) works fine with a 3-tank design.
  - A more variable trading pattern (e.g., LNG sourced from Australia, Indonesia, or Africa, or split deliveries to multiple ports) favors the operational flexibility of a 4-tank design.
  - He recommends reviewing the specific contract and port situation together, doing a proper risk assessment, and only then making a final recommendation to the ship owner — rather than choosing one design as a blanket rule.
